Mainstay user manual

A platform for building and running weather index insurance.

Mainstay creates agro-ecological zones for a country from rainfall data, designs insurance products against that rainfall, prices them, publishes them, quotes them, records policies, and settles claims from the rainfall record at the end of the season. This manual explains what each screen does and gives the exact formulas used for pricing, expected loss, and payouts.

1. What the platform does

A weather index insurance product pays out based on a measured rainfall index, not on a visit to the farm. If the rainfall over a defined period crosses a defined line, the product pays a defined amount. No loss adjuster is involved.

Mainstay uses one rainfall dataset (CHIRPS) for both pricing and settlement, so the amount computed at pricing and the amount computed at settlement use the same code.

2. Workflow overview

A product moves through six stages:

  1. Design — an actuary selects a country, zone map, crop, and season. The platform proposes phases, cover types, and triggers.
  2. Price — for each zone, the platform computes historical payouts, an expected loss, loadings, and a premium rate.
  3. Publish — the actuary freezes the product as a numbered, read-only version and records every assumption behind the rates.
  4. Quote — a field agent supplies a location, crop, season, and sum insured, and receives a premium from the published rate table.
  5. Bind — the agent records the farmers who bought, under a master policy. On premium receipt, the policy becomes active.
  6. Settle — the platform reads the season's rainfall, computes what is owed after the final data is available, and prepares one payment per farmer.

4. Signing in

Enter your username and password on the sign-in screen. The session is held in a secure cookie sent with each request. Use Sign out in the top right to end the session.

Three status indicators appear at the top: api (the web service), db (the database), and worker (the background job runner for fetching, zoning, pricing, and settlement). Green means the component is reachable. Red means a job may fail or a screen may not load.

5. Rainfall data

5.1 Source

Mainstay downloads CHIRPS-2.0, the daily 0.05 degree Africa product, from the Climate Hazards Center at the University of California, Santa Barbara:

https://data.chc.ucsb.edu/products/CHIRPS-2.0/africa_daily/tifs/p05

The platform does not generate rainfall data and does not ship a bundled dataset. It fetches this public dataset on first use and caches it. The cache can be deleted and re-fetched. The public source is authoritative.

5.2 Fetching

On the zoning screen, in the Weather data panel: select a country, set a start year and end year, and press Fetch CHIRPS data. A background job downloads each day, crops it to the country's bounding box, and stores the year. Fetched years appear under Cached years. A year already cached is not downloaded again.

5.3 History depth and the quality flag

You choose how many years to fetch. Five years is the recommended minimum. The record extends back to 1981. A data-quality flag is shown by depth of history, where \(n\) is the number of years used:

\[ \text{flag} = \begin{cases} \text{green} & n \ge 15 \\ \text{amber} & 10 \le n \le 14 \\ \text{red} & n < 10 \end{cases} \]

The flag is advisory and does not block any action. A short history can understate the frequency and size of rare events.

5.4 Provenance

Each cached country-year records: dataset name, source address, country bounding box, pixel grid, number of days retrieved, list of missing days, and the fetch timestamp. Days that CHIRPS has not published are recorded as missing and are not filled with substitute values.

5.5 Zone rainfall index

CHIRPS is a gridded dataset at 0.05 degrees (about 5.5 km per cell). A zone's rainfall on a given day \(d\) is the mean of the grid cells whose centres fall inside the zone polygon:

\[ R_{\text{zone}}(d) = \operatorname{mean}\big\{\, R_{\text{cell}}(d) \;:\; \text{cell centre} \in \text{zone} \,\big\} \]

All farmers in a zone share this single daily series.

6. Zoning

A zone map divides a country into areas with similar rainfall behaviour. There is one zone map per country, shared across all crops and seasons.

6.1 Running a zoning pass

In the Zoning panel, set the year range (the rainfall history used), the number of zones, the rainfall sensitivity (how strongly the grouping uses rainfall behaviour relative to location), and optionally Align zones to districts (each administrative district is assigned whole to its majority zone). Press Run zoning. The background worker performs the clustering and draws the map.

6.2 Features and clustering

For each pixel \(i\), the platform computes two rainfall features across the chosen years: the mean annual total and the coefficient of variation.

\[ m_i = \operatorname{mean}_{y}\big(\text{annual total}_{i,y}\big), \qquad cv_i = \frac{\operatorname{std}_{y}(\text{annual total}_{i,y})}{\operatorname{mean}_{y}(\text{annual total}_{i,y})} \]

The coefficient of variation measures year-to-year variability. Two places with the same average rainfall can fail with different frequency; \(cv\) separates them. Longitude and latitude are centred and scaled by one shared factor so the country's shape is not distorted:

\[ lon_c = lon - \overline{lon}, \qquad lat_c = lat - \overline{lat}, \qquad \text{scale} = \sqrt{\operatorname{mean}\!\big(lon_c^2 + lat_c^2\big)} \]

Each rainfall feature is standardised and multiplied by the sensitivity \(s\), where \(z(x) = \dfrac{x - \bar{x}}{\operatorname{std}(x)}\). The feature vector for pixel \(i\) is:

\[ \mathbf{f}_i = \left[\; \frac{lon_c}{\text{scale}},\;\; \frac{lat_c}{\text{scale}},\;\; z(m_i)\cdot s,\;\; z(cv_i)\cdot s \;\right] \]

These vectors are clustered with k-means into \(k\) zones, using a fixed random seed. The same inputs and seed produce the same zones every time.

6.3 Homogeneity

Each zone has a homogeneity score. Let \(T_i\) be the annual-total series of member cell \(i\), and \(\bar{T}_z\) the zone's mean annual-total series. The homogeneity is the mean correlation of the members with the zone mean, clipped to \([-1, 1]\):

\[ H(z) = \operatorname{mean}_{i \in z}\; \operatorname{corr}\!\big(T_i,\; \bar{T}_z\big) \]

Higher values mean the cells in the zone move together, so one index represents them well. The score needs at least three years of data; below that it is shown as not available.

6.4 Approving

Draft runs are listed so you can compare them. When a map is acceptable, press Approve. This freezes it as a named version (for example KEN-v3) and records who approved it and when. Boundaries cannot be hand-edited in version 1. A different map requires a new run and a new approved version.

7. Crop library

The crop library holds the crop data that products are built from. It is versioned and editable.

7.1 Contents

Open the Crop library tab and select a crop. Each crop has two tables. Growth stages: four stages (establishment, vegetative, flowering, grain filling), each with a duration in days and a water-stress sensitivity weight; the weights sum to 1. Planting windows: one row per country and season, giving the sowing window as a start and end date (MM-DD). You can edit durations, weights, and windows, add and remove planting-window rows, and add a new crop. Press Save as new version to store changes.

7.2 Versions and review

Saving creates a new numbered version. Old versions are immutable. A product priced against version 2 keeps version 2 permanently. Each crop has a reviewed flag. Crops seeded from public sources start unreviewed. They hold starting values from the FAO crop calendars and FAO water-requirement literature. An agronomist should confirm the stages and the local sowing window and save with the reviewed flag set.

7.3 Seeded crops

The platform ships with a library of common African crops: maize, sorghum, pearl millet, finger millet, rice, groundnut, cowpea, common bean, soybean, sunflower, wheat, cotton, and sesame. Each has planting windows for the countries where it is a major crop. They arrive unreviewed. Seeding is additive: it adds crops the database does not have and does not modify crops already present.

7.4 From stages to phases and limits

Stage durations \(d_1, d_2, \dots\) become phase windows by accumulation from the planting date. Stage \(k\) runs from day \(\sum_{j

\[ L_i = \frac{w_i}{\sum_j w_j}\; \cdot\; SI \]

where \(w_i\) is stage \(i\)'s sensitivity weight and \(SI\) is the sum insured. \(L_i\) is the money at risk in phase \(i\).

8. Product design

8.1 Creating a draft

Open the Products tab. In the New product panel choose a country (which determines the zone maps and crops shown), a zone map (an approved map for that country; if none exists, drafting is disabled), a crop (only crops with a planting window for the selected country and season appear), a season, and a sum insured. Press Draft product. A background job reads the zone map and crop, pulls each zone's rainfall history, and proposes phases, cover types, and triggers for every zone. All proposed values can be overridden.

8.2 Cover types

  • Deficit — pays when total rainfall over the stage is too low (drought). Index = total rainfall.
  • Excess — pays when total rainfall over the stage is too high (flood). Index = total rainfall.
  • Dry spell — pays when the longest run of consecutive dry days in the stage is too long. Index = longest dry run in days.

8.3 Index formulas

For deficit and excess cover, the phase index is the sum of daily rainfall over the phase window \(W\) (missing days count as zero):

\[ I = \sum_{d \in W} R_d \quad \text{(mm)} \]

For dry-spell cover, the phase index is the length of the longest run of consecutive days below the dry threshold (2.0 mm by default); a missing day breaks the run:

\[ I = \max\big\{\, \text{length of a run of consecutive days } d \text{ with } R_d < 2.0 \,\big\} \quad \text{(days)} \]

8.4 Payout formula

The payout is linear between the strike \(S\) and the exit \(E\), capped at the phase limit \(L\). The payout fraction \(f\) is 0 at the strike, 1 at the exit, and linear between, using \(\operatorname{clip}(x,0,1) = \min(1, \max(0, x))\).

For deficit cover, \(S > E\) (a drier reading pays more):

\[ f = \operatorname{clip}\!\left(\frac{S - I}{S - E},\; 0,\; 1\right), \qquad \text{payout} = f \cdot L \]

For excess and dry-spell cover, \(E > S\) (a higher reading pays more):

\[ f = \operatorname{clip}\!\left(\frac{I - S}{E - S},\; 0,\; 1\right), \qquad \text{payout} = f \cdot L \]

The year's total payout is the sum of the phase payouts.

Worked example (deficit). Strike \(S = 300\) mm, exit \(E = 150\) mm, limit \(L = 4000\), season rainfall \(I = 210\) mm:

\[ f = \frac{300 - 210}{300 - 150} = \frac{90}{150} = 0.60, \qquad \text{payout} = 0.60 \times 4000 = 2400 \]

8.5 Triggers: percent or absolute

In absolute mode you type the strike and exit in the index's units (mm, or days). In percent mode you type them as a percentage of the phase's normal, the historical mean index for that phase (its \(\text{reference}\)):

\[ S = \frac{\text{strike\%}}{100}\cdot \text{reference}, \qquad E = \frac{\text{exit\%}}{100}\cdot \text{reference} \]

8.6 Proposed triggers

The starting triggers come from the historical distribution of the phase index, by percentile. Let \(P_k\) be the \(k\)-th percentile of the phase's historical index values.

\[ \text{Deficit:} \quad S = P_{15},\; E = P_{5} \qquad\qquad \text{Excess / dry spell:} \quad S = P_{85},\; E = P_{95} \]

This sets the cover to fire in roughly 1 to 2 years in 10 and to reach the full payout only in an extreme year. These are proposals and can be overridden.

8.7 Editing helpers

Balance to sum insured scales the phase limits so they add to the sum insured while keeping their proportions. The stage toggle un-ticks a stage to set its limit to 0 and rebalances the remaining stages, which builds a single-stage variant. Press Recompute payouts after changes.

9. Pricing mathematics

Pricing converts a zone's history of payouts into a premium. This section gives every formula.

9.1 Loss series

Run the product over each historical year to produce one total payout per year: \(x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n\), where \(x_t\) is the total payout in year \(t\) and \(n\) is the number of years.

9.2 Burning cost

\[ BC = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n} x_t \]

Worked example. Losses over 5 years are \([0, 0, 1200, 0, 800]\):

\[ BC = \frac{0 + 0 + 1200 + 0 + 800}{5} = \frac{2000}{5} = 400 \]

9.3 Payout frequency

\[ p = \frac{\big|\{\, t : x_t > 0 \,\}\big|}{n} \]

In the example, two of five years paid, so \(p = 2/5 = 0.40\).

9.4 Severity distribution

Fit a distribution to the positive payouts only (\(x_t > 0\)), by maximum likelihood. Three distributions are available: gamma (default; shape and scale, location fixed at 0), lognormal (sigma and scale, location fixed at 0), and normal (mean and standard deviation). Call the fitted severity \(Y\).

9.5 Modelled expected loss

The modelled expected loss is a frequency-severity Monte Carlo simulation with \(N = 20{,}000\) runs and a fixed seed. For each run \(j\), an occurrence \(O_j\) is drawn from a Bernoulli distribution with probability \(p\), a severity \(Y_j\) is drawn from the fitted distribution, and the loss is \(O_j\) times that severity clipped to \([0, SI]\):

\[ O_j \sim \text{Bernoulli}(p), \qquad Y_j \sim \hat{F}_Y, \qquad \text{loss}_j = O_j \cdot \min\!\big(\max(Y_j, 0),\; SI\big) \]
\[ EL_{\text{model}} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N} \text{loss}_j \]

Because occurrence and severity are independent in the simulation, this equals:

\[ EL_{\text{model}} = p \cdot \mathbb{E}\!\big[\min(Y, SI)\big] \]

The fixed seed makes this reproducible. If there are fewer than 2 positive payouts, or the positive payouts have no spread, the distribution cannot be fitted and the modelled expected loss falls back to the burning cost: \(EL_{\text{model}} = BC\). A Q-Q plot of the sorted positive payouts against the fitted quantiles is shown so the fit can be checked.

9.6 Technical expected loss

The technical expected loss is the larger of the two, and is the expected loss used for pricing:

\[ EL = \max\big(BC,\; EL_{\text{model}}\big) \]

9.7 Loadings

Loadings are added on top of the expected loss to reach the gross premium. Each loading has one of three bases: pct_el (a percentage of the expected loss), pct_gross (a percentage of the gross premium), or flat (a fixed amount per policy). The default loadings are:

LoadingBasisValue
Uncertainty marginpct_el15%
Admin and operationspct_gross10%
Distribution commissionpct_gross15%
Profit and capitalpct_gross10%

All loadings are editable.

9.8 Gross premium and rate

Let \(EL\) be the technical expected loss, and let each loading have value \(v\). Form the base from the expected loss, the percent-of-EL loadings, and the flat loadings:

\[ \text{base} = EL \;+\; \sum_{i \in \text{pct\_el}} EL\cdot\frac{v_i}{100} \;+\; \sum_{k \in \text{flat}} v_k \]

Let \(g\) be the sum of the percent-of-gross loading fractions:

\[ g = \sum_{j \in \text{pct\_gross}} \frac{v_j}{100} \]

The gross premium grosses up for the percent-of-gross loadings, which requires \(g < 1\) (otherwise the premium is undefined and the platform refuses to price). The premium rate is the gross premium as a percentage of the sum insured:

\[ G = \frac{\text{base}}{1 - g}, \qquad \text{rate} = \frac{100\,G}{SI}\;\;(\%) \]

9.9 Worked example (end to end)

Take a technical expected loss \(EL = 400\) and a sum insured \(SI = 10{,}000\), with the default loadings. The only percent-of-EL loading is the 15% uncertainty margin, so:

\[ \text{base} = 400 + 400\cdot\frac{15}{100} = 400 + 60 = 460 \]

The percent-of-gross loadings are \(10\% + 15\% + 10\% = 35\%\), so \(g = 0.35\):

\[ G = \frac{460}{1 - 0.35} = \frac{460}{0.65} = 707.69, \qquad \text{rate} = \frac{100 \times 707.69}{10{,}000} = 7.08\% \]

The gross premium is 707.69 on a sum insured of 10,000, a rate of 7.08%.

9.10 Loading amounts

The amount of each loading, for the breakdown shown on screen:

\[ \text{pct\_el: } EL\cdot\frac{v}{100}, \qquad \text{flat: } v, \qquad \text{pct\_gross: } G\cdot\frac{v}{100} \]

Using the example (\(EL = 400\), \(G = 707.69\)):

Uncertainty margin (15% of EL):     400 * 0.15    = 60.00
Admin (10% of gross):               707.69 * 0.10 = 70.77
Distribution (15% of gross):        707.69 * 0.15 = 106.15
Profit and capital (10% of gross):  707.69 * 0.10 = 70.77

9.11 Consistency of pricing and settlement

The same function computes the historical payout table used in pricing and the live payout used in settlement. The engine that prices a product settles it.

10. Publishing

10.1 What publishing does

With a draft open, the actuary presses Publish product and confirms. The platform re-prices every zone from the current terms, so the stored rate equals the rate shown on screen. It then freezes the product as a numbered, read-only version and writes an audit record.

10.2 Validation

In every zone, the phase limits must add up to the sum insured within a tolerance of 1 unit or 0.5% of the sum insured, whichever is larger:

\[ \Big|\, \textstyle\sum_i L_i - SI \,\Big| \;\le\; \max\big(1.0,\; 0.005 \cdot SI\big) \]

If any zone fails, publishing stops and lists all failing zones.

10.3 Versioning

Products are versioned by country, crop, and season. The version number increments by 1 each time. The identifier has the form {country}-{crop}-{season}-v{version}, for example KEN-maize-long_rains-v1. There is no edit or delete. A change means a new version. Quoting uses only the newest version's rates.

10.4 Stored rate table

For each zone, the published product stores: premium rate, gross premium, expected loss, burning cost, technical expected loss, and data-quality flag.

10.5 Assumption sheet

Every published product stores an assumption sheet recording every input behind the rates:

  • Product: country, crop, crop-library version, crop source, whether the crop was reviewed, season, planting date, sum insured, pricing years.
  • Dataset: dataset name, product, source address, and for each year, the fetch time and the count of missing days.
  • Zone map: name, number of zones, years used, rainfall sensitivity, whether aligned to districts, who approved it, and when.
  • Method: the index definition, the expected-loss rule \(EL = \max(BC, EL_{\text{model}})\), the severity distribution, the gross-up formula, and the full loading list.

A separate audit record stores the action, who published, when, the source draft, the distribution and loadings, which zones were overridden in the session, and the number of zones. A printable version can be exported by printing to PDF. It shows the crop as reviewed or unreviewed, lists the rate and quality flag for every zone, and states that the rates were produced before any external benchmark was consulted.

11. Quoting

Quoting is a lookup against the published rate table. Field agents use a separate lightweight page.

11.1 Process

An agent supplies a location (a map pin or a picked village), a crop, a season, and a sum insured. The platform converts the location to a point (a picked village uses the representative interior point of its administrative district), finds the zone containing that point by a point-in-polygon test over the product's frozen zone map, looks up the published product for the country, crop, and season, reads the zone's premium rate, and computes the premium.

11.2 Premium formula

\[ \text{premium} = \frac{\text{rate}}{100}\cdot SI \]

The response includes a reference number of the form Q-{country}-{yymmdd}-{5 hex}, a plain-language cover summary, a validity date, the data-quality flag, and the exact product version used.

11.3 No coverage

If no product is published for that country, crop, and season, or the point falls outside every mapped zone, the agent is told coverage is not available. Each such case is logged as a demand signal with a reason (no_product or outside_coverage). An agent sees only their own quotes.

12. Binding (policies)

12.1 Two-level register

Every sale is a master policy over a schedule of farmers. A single sale is a master with one farmer. A partner sale is a master with a partner name over many farmers. To bind, operations records the farmers under a master. Each entry is priced from its originating quote if one exists (the quote must exist, match the product, and have been created by the same user), otherwise from the published rate for the zone. An individual sale must have exactly one farmer. A partner sale requires a partner name. The master starts in draft status.

12.2 Statuses

draft  -> active     (only via premium receipt)
active -> expired
active -> settled
draft  -> expired    (lapsed unpaid)

12.3 Premium receipt

Mainstay records money, it does not move money. Premium is collected in existing company or partner channels. Recording a receipt (a reference and a date) on a draft policy sets its status to active. Only a draft can be activated this way.

12.4 Sales cut-off

To limit anti-selection, a policy is stamped at bind time with the next planting season whose sales window is still open. The window closes a set number of days before planting (14 by default):

\[ \text{sales cut-off date} = \text{plant\_start} - \text{cutoff\_days} \]

If this year's window has closed, the policy is stamped with next year's season. A late buyer is covered for the season ahead, not the season already running. A product can override the cut-off days.

12.5 PII and encryption

The farmer fields stored are name, phone, gender, and an optional national ID. The phone is also the payment address. Name, phone, email, and national ID are encrypted at rest using Fernet (AES-128 in CBC mode with an HMAC for integrity). Gender is stored in clear. The encryption key comes from the environment variable AEZ_PII_KEY. In production the host generates and holds this key. The development fallback key is deterministic and is not secret; it must not be used in production. If the key is lost, the data cannot be recovered. If the key is leaked, the at-rest encryption gives no protection. Farmer details are decrypted only in role-scoped views. An agent sees only policies they created. Operations and admin see all policies.

12.6 Policy document

Operations can produce a printable policy document. It states the cover and includes a basis-risk clause: because the payout follows a rainfall index rather than the individual field, it may pay when little was lost and may not pay when a loss occurred. The legal wording is a placeholder for the insurer's counsel to complete. It is not legal advice and must be reviewed and approved by the insurer's lawyers and regulator before commercial issue.

13. Season and settlement

13.1 Season dashboard

Operations opens the Season tab to follow a live product phase by phase. Settlement uses the same code path as pricing, run over the live season's rainfall.

13.2 Phase states

  • upcoming — the stage window has not finished. No index or payout is shown.
  • provisional — the window has finished but the final data has not settled. A figure is shown, labelled provisional, and is not paid on.
  • settled — the final data is available. The figure is safe to pay.

13.3 Final-data date

CHIRPS publishes final data about three weeks after each month ends. A phase becomes settleable on:

\[ \text{final\_ready} = \text{month\_end}\big(\text{last day of } W\big) + \text{lag\_days} \]

The default \(\text{lag\_days}\) is 21. A product can override it. The lag is printed into the product terms.

13.4 Persistence

Only settled phases are written to the settlement record. The record is unique per product, season, zone, and phase, and it does not overwrite. The first final computation stands. A scheduled sweep refreshes the season's rainfall so newly published final days are picked up.

14. Payout run

14.1 Readiness

A payout run is ready only when every phase of every zone that holds policies is settled. Until then, the screen lists the pending zones.

14.2 Building the run

The platform builds a review without saving: totals, the count of farmers being paid against the count bound, a per-zone table, and the five largest individual payments. Only active policies are paid.

14.3 Per-farmer amount

Every farmer in a zone shares one index and one rate. The zone rate is the zone's total settled payout divided by the product's sum insured, and each farmer's amount is that rate times their own sum insured:

\[ \text{zone\_rate} = \frac{\text{zone settled payout total}}{\text{product } SI}, \qquad \text{farmer amount} = \text{zone\_rate} \times SI_{\text{farmer}} \]

14.4 Anomaly check

A zone is flagged when its settled payout exceeds 3 times the expected loss it was priced at. The flag is advisory; it prompts a review before release and does not block it.

\[ \text{flag zone if} \quad \text{zone settled payout total} \;>\; 3 \times EL_{\text{zone}} \]

14.5 Release

Release is a one-way lock and asks for confirmation twice. On release, the platform writes an immutable run record and one line per paid farmer, and sets every active policy in the season to settled. A season is closed once, and a farmer is paid once per run. The action is written to the audit trail. The payout file has fixed columns and a fixed order:

policy_number, phone, zone, amount, evidence_reference

The phone is decrypted only into this outbound file. The money is recorded, not moved; the file is sent to the existing payment rails.

15. Operations (demand signals)

The Operations tab is a read-only view of demand signals: the cases where an agent requested cover the platform could not quote. Reasons are no_product (no product for that country, crop, and season) and outside_coverage (the point fell outside the mapped area). This shows where cover was requested but did not exist.

16. Users

The Users tab is admin-only. It creates accounts and assigns a role (admin, actuary, agronomist, operations, agent). Roles gate the screens and the API. Admin is a superuser. The last active admin cannot be deactivated or demoted.

17. Open standards and open source

Mainstay uses open data, open formats, and open-source software throughout.

Open data. Rainfall comes from CHIRPS, a public dataset published by the Climate Hazards Center at UC Santa Barbara. The exact files used can be re-downloaded from the public source.

Open formats. GeoTIFF for the rainfall raster; GeoJSON for zone polygons; compressed NumPy arrays (.npz) for cached rainfall; JSON for provenance and audit records; ISO 3166 three-letter codes for countries.

Open-source stack. Python with FastAPI (backend); NumPy and SciPy (numerics and distribution fitting); scikit-learn (k-means clustering); shapely and rasterio (spatial operations); PostgreSQL with PostGIS (database); Celery with Redis (background jobs); React with MapLibre (frontend and map), with OpenFreeMap tiles.

Open cryptography. PBKDF2-HMAC-SHA256 for password hashing; Fernet (AES-128-CBC with HMAC) for farmer PII.

Reproducibility. The input is public, the code is open, and the random steps use fixed seeds. The same rainfall and settings produce the same zones, and the same history and seed produce the same price. A result can be re-run and checked. The full stack self-hosts with docker compose up.

18. Limitations

Basis risk. The index follows a rainfall grid, not an individual field. It can pay when little was lost and can miss a real loss. The zoning homogeneity score measures this, and the policy document states it.

Short history. A small number of years can understate the frequency and size of rare events. The data-quality flag warns when history is thin; green requires 15 years.

CHIRPS resolution. CHIRPS is a satellite-and-gauge estimate at about 5.5 km. It is an estimate, not a gauge in every field, and leans more on satellite where ground gauges are sparse.

Legal wording. The policy document carries placeholder terms. It is not legal advice and must be completed and approved by the insurer's counsel and regulator before commercial use.

Key custody. Farmer PII is protected only while the encryption key is kept secret. In production, the host must generate and hold the key. The development fallback key must not be used.

Version 1 scope. Fixed-calendar phase windows; one payment per farmer at season end; no detrending of the rainfall history; no tail loading factors; no separate checker before publishing. These are documented choices.

Recommended validation. Run a known drought season through the zones and triggers and confirm the payouts fall where the losses occurred (a back-test). This is the strongest check before relying on a rate commercially.

20. Glossary

Basis risk. The difference between what the index measures and what a farmer actually lost.
Burning cost. The mean of the historical annual payouts.
CHIRPS. The public rainfall dataset used, from UC Santa Barbara.
Cover type. Deficit (too little rain), excess (too much rain), or dry spell (a long run of dry days).
Expected loss. The average cost of the risk. The technical expected loss is the larger of the burning cost and the modelled figure.
Homogeneity. The mean correlation of a zone's cells with the zone mean.
Loading. An amount added to the expected loss to reach a premium (margin, admin, commission, profit).
Master policy and schedule. A master policy over a list of one or more farmers.
Provisional and settled. A provisional figure uses preliminary rainfall and is not paid on; a settled figure uses final rainfall and is paid.
Strike and exit. The two payout lines. Payout is 0 at the strike and equals the phase limit at the exit.
Sum insured. The cover amount.
Zone map. The grouping of a country into zones, one per country, frozen as a named version.

21. Appendix: running and deploying

Run the full stack locally (requires Docker):

docker compose up

The API is at http://localhost:8000 (health check at /health). The three status indicators show the API, database, and worker.

Deploy online with Render, from the blueprint file. The app redeploys on every push to main. On first setup, connect the repository, let Render create the database, cache, backend, and frontend, then set the frontend's API URL and the API's allowed origins. After that, a push is a deploy.

The environment secret that matters most in production is AEZ_PII_KEY, which encrypts farmer PII. The host must generate and hold a strong value. The development fallback must not be used.
